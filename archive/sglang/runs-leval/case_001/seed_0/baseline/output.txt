This paper conducts an extensive empirical study of conventional regularization techniques—L1/L2 weight penalties, weight clipping, dropout, batch‑normalization (BN), and entropy regularization—on four widely used policy‑optimization algorithms (A2C, TRPO, PPO, SAC) across nine continuous‑control benchmarks (MuJoCo + RoboSchool). The authors present a clear experimental protocol, evaluate each regularizer in isolation, and systematically analyze their impact on training performance, sample efficiency, and generalization to unseen trajectories. The main empirical findings—especially that simple L2 weight decay consistently boosts performance (often surpassing entropy regularization), that BN/dropout only help the off‑policy SAC, and that regularizing the policy network alone is sufficient—are intriguing and potentially valuable for practitioners.

**Strengths**

1. **Comprehensiveness**: The study covers a broad spectrum of algorithms, tasks, and hyper‑parameter regimes, providing a nuanced view of when each regularizer helps.
2. **Methodological rigor**: The authors report mean returns over five seeds, define clear criteria for “improvement” and “hurting,” and perform additional experiments varying network size and hyper‑parameters.
3. **Insightful analysis**: The discussion on why BN/dropout hurt on‑policy methods and the generalization experiments add depth beyond mere performance tables.
4. **Practical relevance**: Findings directly inform RL practitioners about inexpensive tricks that can be applied to standard baselines.

**Weaknesses and Questions**

1. **Statistical significance**: Improvement/hurting thresholds and standard‑deviation bands are used, but no formal hypothesis tests or confidence intervals are reported. With only five seeds, the variance of returns in some hard tasks is large; statistical tests would strengthen the claims.
2. **Hyper‑parameter dependence**: Regularization strengths are tuned per algorithm–environment pair, but the search space is limited. It remains unclear how robust the conclusions are to broader or different ranges, and whether an automated tuning framework (e.g., Bayesian optimization) would alter the relative rankings.
3. **Scope of algorithms**: Only four policy‑optimization methods are considered. Modern off‑policy methods such as DDPG, TD3, or SAC variants could behave differently under dropout/BN, especially given their separate actor–critic architectures.
4. **Dropout/BN implementation**: The paper notes that dropout/BN are used in training mode during updates but test mode during sampling. The impact of this mismatch on on‑policy algorithms is plausible, but an ablation varying dropout rates or BN momentum could quantify the sensitivity.
5. **Weight clipping vs. gradient clipping**: The study focuses on weight clipping, yet gradient clipping is a common stabilizer in RL. A comparison would clarify whether the observed benefits are specific to weight clipping or part of a broader “norm‑based regularization” family.
6. **Reproducibility**: The manuscript references codebases but does not provide a dedicated repository or scripts. Making the experimental pipeline public would greatly aid replication.

- Compare weight clipping with gradient clipping and other norm‑based techniques (e.g., spectral normalization).
