This paper proposes Variational Network Quantization (VNQ), a Bayesian framework that jointly performs weight pruning and few‑bit quantization. By replacing the standard log‑uniform prior with a continuous multi‑spike‑and‑slab prior, the authors encourage posterior samples to cluster around a small set of target values (e.g., ternary codes). The resulting variational posterior is a fully factorized Gaussian, and the KL term is approximated by a weighted sum of shifted versions of the Sparse‑VD KL. After training, a simple threshold on the learned dropout rates prunes weights, while remaining weights are hard‑quantized to the nearest codebook entry. Experiments on LeNet‑5 (MNIST) and DenseNet (CIFAR‑10) demonstrate that VNQ can achieve high sparsity with negligible loss in accuracy, without fine‑tuning.

**Strengths**

1. **Novelty** – The idea of a multi‑modal quantizing prior is elegant and directly addresses the joint pruning‑quantization objective.  
2. **Methodological soundness** – The use of local reparameterization, additive noise, and a principled KL approximation is well motivated and builds on recent Sparse‑VD work.  
3. **Empirical results** – The method works on both small and medium‑scale networks, and the authors provide visualizations of weight distributions.  
4. **Clarity** – The manuscript is generally well written; equations are clearly numbered and most derivations are traceable.

**Weaknesses / Questions**

1. **KL approximation quality** – The approximation (Eq. 16) is only loosely validated (Fig. 4). How sensitive are the results to the choice of τ and the specific window functions? Would a neural‑network surrogate or higher‑order Taylor expansion improve performance?  
2. **Hyper‑parameter sensitivity** – The paper relies on several ad‑hoc settings (warm‑up schedule, clipping bounds, gradient stopping). A systematic ablation (e.g., varying β warm‑up, or the clipping threshold) would strengthen the claim that VNQ is robust.  
3. **Compression metrics** – While the authors rightfully point out the difficulty of comparing compression rates, a brief quantitative comparison (e.g., bits per weight after pruning/quantization) would help contextualize the sparsity levels reported.  
4. **Codebook learning stability** – The authors mention that learning the quantization level a can collapse early. A more detailed discussion of the learning dynamics (perhaps a plot of a(t) during training) would be useful.  
5. **Scalability** – Experiments are limited to LeNet‑5 and a 76‑layer DenseNet. How does VNQ scale to larger models (e.g., ResNet‑50) or to networks with batch‑norm, where the additive noise assumption may be violated?  
6. **Comparison to other Bayesian methods** – The paper cites several Bayesian compression works but does not directly compare to them in a unified framework. A side‑by‑side evaluation would clarify the benefit of the quantizing prior.

**Suggestions**

- Provide an ablation study on the KL approximation and on the hyper‑parameters (β, τ, clipping).  
- Include a simple compression‑ratio table (bits per weight before/after) for both datasets.  
- Add a small experiment with a non‑ternary codebook (e.g., 5‑level) to demonstrate flexibility.  
- Clarify the role of the “shadow weight” and show its impact on convergence.  
- Discuss potential extensions to layer‑wise or structured sparsity (e.g., filter pruning).

Overall, the paper presents a promising approach that bridges Bayesian inference and hardware‑friendly quantization. Addressing the above points would significantly strengthen the manuscript.
