\[
x>1,\qquad y>1
\]

and

\[
\log_x (y^x)=10 ,\qquad \log_y(x^{4y})=10.
\]

---

### 1.  Rewrite the logarithmic equations

\[
\log_x(y^x)=10\quad\Longrightarrow\quad y^x=x^{10},
\]

\[
\log_y(x^{4y})=10\quad\Longrightarrow\quad x^{4y}=y^{10}.
\]

Take natural logarithms:

\[
x\ln y=10\ln x \tag{1}
\]
\[
4y\ln x=10\ln y \tag{2}
\]

Let  

\[
a=\ln x>0,\qquad b=\ln y>0.
\]

Then (1) and (2) become

\[
x\,b=10\,a, \tag{A}
\]
\[
4y\,a=10\,b. \tag{B}
\]

---

### 2.  Eliminate \(a\) and \(b\)

From (A)  

\[
b=\frac{10a}{x}. \tag{3}
\]

Insert (3) into (B):

\[
4y\,a=10\,\frac{10a}{x}\;\Longrightarrow\;4y=\frac{100}{x}
\]
\[
\Longrightarrow\;4xy=100\;\Longrightarrow\;xy=25.
\]

Therefore the product \(xy\) is fixed at

\[
\boxed{25}.
\]

---

(There indeed exist \(x,y>1\) satisfying the original logarithmic equations—any pair with \(xy=25\) that meets \(y^x=x^{10}\) will automatically satisfy the second equation.  The calculation shows that the product \(xy\) must be \(25\) irrespective of the particular values of \(x\) and \(y\).)
