If the satellite’s speed falls below the speed that a circular orbit at the present radius requires, the satellite is no longer in a stable circular orbit.  
Its kinetic energy is reduced, so the satellite’s *total* orbital energy becomes more negative.  Consequently the semi‑major axis of the orbit shrinks: the satellite falls to a smaller radius, the orbit becomes elliptical, and the time it takes to make one revolution (the period) becomes shorter.  

---

### 1.  What does a circular orbit require?

For a circular orbit the radial acceleration must be exactly the centripetal acceleration needed for a circular path:

\[
\frac{v^{2}}{r} = \frac{GM_{\oplus}}{r^{2}}
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
v_{\rm circ}= \sqrt{\frac{GM_{\oplus}}{r}} .
\]

The angular velocity is \(\omega=v/r\), and the period is

\[
T = \frac{2\pi r}{v} = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{r^{3}}{GM_{\oplus}}}\; .
\]

This follows from the same relation we will use for the tiny changes we are considering.

---

### 2.  What happens when the speed decreases?

Assume that the satellite initially moves at the correct circular speed \(v_c\) but then a drag force, mis‑aimed thrust, or a touchdown manoeuvre reduces its speed by a tiny amount \(\Delta v\).  
When the satellite is released from the manoeuvre its speed is

\[
v_{\text{initial}} = v_c - \Delta v \;<\; v_c .
\]

Because the satellite’s speed is now *smaller* than that which is needed for a circular orbit at the same radius, the **centripetal force is smaller** than the gravitational attraction that keeps it circular.  
The “excess” inward pull causes the satellite to accelerate toward the Earth.  It will not stay in a circular orbit; it starts to spiral inward. 

---

### 3.  Energy picture

The total specific orbital energy of a two‑body orbit is

\[
E=\frac{v^{2}}{2}-\frac{GM_{\oplus}}{r}\, .
\]

In a circular orbit \(E = -\,\frac{GM_{\oplus}}{2r}\).  When the speed is reduced by \(\Delta v\),

\[
E_{\text{new}}
=\frac{(v_c - \Delta v)^{2}}{2}-\frac{GM_{\oplus}}{r}
= -\,\frac{GM_{\oplus}}{2r} - v_c\,\Delta v + \frac{(\Delta v)^{2}}{2},
\]

which is **more negative** than the original energy.  
For a bound orbit the energy is related to the semi‑major axis \(a\):

\[
E = -\,\frac{GM_{\oplus}}{2a}.
\]

A more negative total energy means a *smaller* semi‑major axis, i.e. the orbit shrinks.  
Thus the orbit becomes elliptical with a perigee lower than the original radius and a still‑greater eccentricity (the apogee is the original radius).

---

### 4.  Consequences for the orbital radius and period

Because the orbital radius shrinks, the orbital period shortens according to Kepler’s third law:

\[
T_{\text{new}} = 2\pi \sqrt{\frac{a^3}{GM_{\oplus}}} .
\]

Since \(a < r\) (in the original circular orbit), \(T_{\text{new}} < T_{\text{original}}\).

If we suppose that the satellite somehow adjusts its speed to the new circular value at the new radius (e.g. by a last‑moment retaking), the same relation holds:

\[
T = 2\pi r_{\text{new}}/v_{\text{new}},
\qquad
v_{\text{new}} = \sqrt{\frac{GM_{\oplus}}{r_{\text{new}}}} ,
\]

so that \(T_{\text{new}} = 2\pi\sqrt{r_{\text{new}}^3/(GM_{\oplus})}\).

---

### 5.  Bottom line

* **Orbital radius**: decreases (the satellite radially moves inward).
* **Period of revolution**: decreases (since the orbit is smaller and Kepler’s law gives a shorter period).

The satellite therefore spirals into a progressively lower orbit, with a shorter, faster repeat time, until it eventually hits the Earth or any other obstacle it encounters.  The underlying physics hinges on conservation of angular momentum and the fact that the satellite’s kinetic energy falls below the level required for a circular orbit at that altitude.
