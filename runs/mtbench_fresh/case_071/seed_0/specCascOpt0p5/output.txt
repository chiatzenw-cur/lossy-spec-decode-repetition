## 2.  Fiscal Policy – How Policy Shapes Income & Prices

### 2.1  What the government is doing

| Type | Typical effect | Example explanation |
|------|-----------------|---------------------|
| **Direct Spending** | Increase aggregate consumption, higher GDP, higher employment |
| **Tax Cuts** | Raise disposable income, stimulates spending → higher GDP & investment |
| **Transfer Payments** | Strengthens marginal propensity to consume; improves psychological safety of households; may down‑shift inflation (if it is not pegged to monetary spending) |
| **Subsidies & Infrastructure** | Preserve local output → better basic conditions; often yields short‑run to intermediate‑term high‑output gains |

### 2.2  Why it matters – the “Fiscal Home‑Cycle”

- **Multiplier**: When a government spends *$1 billion* of labor‑wage‑rich economy **(a multiplier ≈ 1.5–2.0)**, the GDP rises \(1.5–2.0\) billion.  
- **Crowding‑out – Lower Borrowing Demand**: If government expenditures drive up debt, potentially crowd‑out private investment.  
- **Crowding‑out – Higher Borrowing**: Higher debt creates a *crowding‑out* mechanism (more real). In high‑inflation early periods, the ‘crowding‑out’ may be minimal.

### 2.3  How it enacts the opposite of the Phillips curve

| Tactic | Resulting Inflation | Resulting unemployment |
|--------|---------------------|------------------------|
| **Fiscal stimulus** (boom‑boom) | ↑ inflation (boosted by high aggregate demand). | ↓ unemployment (decreases). |
| **Fiscal tightening** (tax cuts) | ↓ inflation (decrease). | ↑ unemployment (increase). |

> **Implication**: Fiscal policy offers a *direct lever* to shift **both** *output* and *inflation*. Without long‑run constraints, it can shift one side of the curve.

## 3.  Monetary Policy – The Bank’s Roller

### 3.1  The tools & its key constraints

| Tool | Typical effect | Net effect | Target for inflation |
|------|-----------------|------------|----------------------|
| **Discount Rate (DRA)** | Lower open‑market contract → higher borrowing cost | Lower yields (no‑index for short-run) | 2‑3 %‑10 % |
| **Interest Rate (Interbank rate)** | Changes the cost of capping costs, expands/recontracting | Short‑run changes → output, inflation | 2‑5 % |
| **Shocks (quantitative)** | Annually or over 10‑year outlook | Sparks flows of future inflation > 2 % | < 2 % |

### 3.2  What does the policy change?  

- **Expansionary policy**: Lower rates → cheaper credit; higher rates → more risk of low‑income, high‑cost unemployment.  
- **Contractionary policy**: Higher rates → more in‑elastic; >10 % price inflation = significant filters.  

### 3.3  The 2‑% rule

| Policy | Effect | Rationale |
|--------|--------|----------|
| **Quick‑response** | \(\Delta\) inflation by 1‑2 % per 4‑month period r.t. | When market changes, “expected” price movement changes about 7‑10 % change. |
| **Long‑run** | 2‑2 % inflation works out with be seen (Primarily 0.6 extra for each 1‑2 % growth). | (Fair‑Today evidence). |

### 3.4  Fiscal‑monetary shock synergy

The policy “committee” we observe:

1. Fiscal: If the Fed rates low (5 % QE), it can push high inflation to GDP.  
2. Monetary: If the Fed draws strong negative conditions, inflation increases.  

> **Why**: In the short‑run, *the fiscal side* can stimulate base demand; *the monetary side* can quell the imperative. The specifics result in net **negative or positive** inflation quality.

- *Fiscal + Monetary* synergy for GDP and inflation experiences the *trade‑off*;
